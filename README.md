# Tiger Tower I

This is a public repository for the Kerr Engineering Club’s vertical profiling float: Tiger Tower I. Tiger Tower I was built for the purpose of completing the 4th (ranger-class) task in the Houston Marine Advanced Technology Education (MATE) Remotely Operated Vehicle Competition.

Task Preview: https://20693798.fs1.hubspotusercontent-na1.net/hubfs/20693798/2026/2026%20MATE%20Floats%20Preview%20Mission_RANGER_PIONEER_FINAL_Updated_10_1.pdf

This repository is primarily meant for current members of the **Kerr Engineering Club** who are interested in the MATE ROV competition and are also interested in working on a vertical profiling float. Tiger Tower I is **incomplete** as of the 2026 competition, so this is meant to be documentation for the float to complete and improve upon it. However, non-members who are participating in the competition can feel free to use this repository as a source of information about building a vertical profiling float.

There is also a logbook that details the process of developing Tiger Tower I, which goes into further detail about the tools and decisions made during its design and construction.

Below is the documentation for the main components of the float. This was written after the competition to provide a more comprehensive and streamlined explanation of Tiger Tower I.

## Goal
The goal was to create a vertical profiling float that can, without manual input, submerge to a given depth of a large body of water, remain stationary for 30 seconds, record the water pressure and depth between 5-second intervals, re-emerge, and wirelessly transmit data to a computer.
## The Chassis
### Body
The internals of the float are contained in a 4-inch nominal schedule 40 PVC pipe. The length of this PVC pipe was originally 56 cm before being shortened to 50 cm. (Based on measurement, the inner diameter was approximately 3.956 inches, and the outer diameter was approximately 4.504 inches.)

The reason I chose a PVC pipe was simple: it was readily available, sturdy, and buoyant. The buoyancy of a sealed PVC pipe suits our water-based buoyancy engine. The shape of the PVC pipe was cylindrical, which means that the buoyant force acting upon the float would be uniform, and not biased on one side or another. One key drawback, however, is that PVC pipes are opaque, meaning electronics inspection will require the unsealing of the float. Additionally, the 2026 competition required a safety handle that the ROV could use to retrieve it. While a design was not made in time, it was likely that a thin, 3D-printed ring with 4 support beams attached to the chassis would’ve been used to maintain the uniform shape while minimizing the additional surface area.

That particular size of the PVC pipe was chosen since it allowed for much leeway in the development of the float’s components. The long-term plan was to develop the parts, then cut the chassis down to the minimum size possible. However, the sheer size of the pipe meant that even with the largest syringes available, the float would never have been able to sink.

### Compartment Layout
The float was separated into two main compartments: the electronics and the buoyancy engine. The electronics were kept at the top. This is mainly because vertical profiling floats require radio waves to communicate wirelessly. However, the range of radio waves can be adversely affected underwater. While the float is on the surface, some amount of the bottom of the float must be submerged, meaning that the electronics should be on the top part of the float for the sake of communication. This meant that the buoyancy engine was at the bottom. Additionally, a ballast was planned to also be near the bottom of the float to provide stability.
## Buoyancy Engine
### Chosen Type of Buoyancy Engine
While a buoyancy engine was not required for the completion of this task, it was encouraged by the competition. There are two main types of buoyancy engines we could have used: an air-based buoyancy engine and a water-based buoyancy engine. 

An air-based buoyancy engine would require an internal air tank, a bladder to contain the air, and a pump. The float must also experience negative buoyancy by default. The bladder must also be exposed outside the float. While the bladder is deflated, the float sinks. To go back up, air could be pumped into the bladder to give the float more volume, giving the float positive buoyancy.

A water-based buoyancy engine would require a syringe and a motor-powered linear actuator. A syringe can be used in place of a pump and bladder because it can store, intake, and expel water with the help of a linear actuator. The float would begin at positive buoyancy before the syringe draws in water, causing it to sink. Once the float needs to surface, the syringe can expel the water to bring it back to positive buoyancy. For its simplicity, we picked the water-based buoyancy engine.
### Syringe
I had the option of using a large syringe or multiple small syringes. While a large syringe could’ve easily been used, I had to consider the size of the syringe relative to both the electronics compartment and the mechanism needed to push and pull the syringe. Additionally, the more water is taken in, the more power the motor would need. Finally, the float would only need just enough water for it to sink and float. Sinking and rising slowly would’ve been beneficial in finding neutral buoyancy, which was necessary in keeping the float still. Therefore, I decided to use three 60 mL syringes, which stored a total of 180 mL. In hindsight, this was far from being enough to sink the float due to the size of the chassis.
### Linear Actuator
The linear actuator, in retrospect, easily took the most time out of this project. Despite it still being a non-functional state, I believe that it was a necessary sacrifice in the long term due to the potential for mass-producing a linear actuator. The reason why I decided to make a linear actuator instead of buying one was simple: cost. While the cost of linear actuators isn’t insurmountable, our club does not currently have the funds to casually buy an actuator. The cost of one relative to every other component of the float would mean that I would have to design the entire float around it. While that is not necessarily a downside, making adjustments to the float’s design could become restrictive by buying an actuator. Moreover, the power that a linear actuator offers is simply too much for what is required for the buoyancy engine. The lower I managed to find was able to exert a force of 40 pounds, which could actually end up compromising the float if I did not implement limit switches for whatever reason. I would need no more than 5 pounds to extract the maximum amount of water out of the largest syringe I could find. Therefore, I found it better long-term to design and 3D-print parts of a weaker linear actuator that can be mass-produced and adjusted to my liking.

The actuator itself had an 8:1 gear ratio, which was presumably sufficient for expelling and drawing in 180 mL of water. The gearbox and gears were 3D-printed with PLA, while the arm and its container were made out of PVC. The PVC arm contained a cork with an indent and a hole made to fit an M7 nut and bolt. This would let the motor rotate the bolt, which would extend the arm up to a given stroke length while exerting force. Since the barrel of our syringes was 5 inches, the stroke length had to be 5 inches as well, which meant that I needed to reserve more than 10 inches (25 cm) of space for the buoyancy engine. For future attempts at making (or buying) a linear actuator, I would prioritize reserving space above all else, because my design was too long and wide.
## Electronics
### Circuitry
The electronics compartment had 2 sections. The first section, located at the top of the float, would have had the batteries, the switch, the fuse, the microcontroller, the motor controller, and the pressure sensor. The float itself was powered by 4 AA NiMH batteries, which possessed 4.8V in total. There was wiring that led to the second section, which contained the motor for the buoyancy engine and limit switches for the linear actuator. 

The linear actuators required limit switches to prevent over-extension or over-retraction without tripping the fuse. This helps control the stroke range of the actuator. Most linear actuators possess power diodes so that if the arm triggers a switch, the arm cannot go past the switch and can only go in the reverse direction. However, we had no diodes that could handle more than 1 amp, meaning that I had to hook up the limit switches to the digital pins of a microcontroller. I then programmed them so that if there was a signal on the digital pin, then the function to extend in a given direction would be disabled.

At one point, there were also plans to include an SD card module. This ended up being discarded due to a lack of time and necessity, as the Pico had enough storage to store data for at least two vertical profiles.
### Microcontroller
The microcontroller I chose was the Raspberry Pi Pico W. This was because the microcontroller was compact, had innate Wi-Fi and Bluetooth capabilities, and possessed the computing power needed for the task. The Raspberry Pi Pico W also has an on-board buck boost converter, which can convert a range of voltage inputs to 5V, which can then be used to power a variety of electronics on the float.
### Data Recording and Transmission
The pressure sensor is the main way for data to be recorded. The sensor needed to be hooked up to a power source and an analog pin to return readable data. This sensor would then emit a signal between 0V and 3.3V, which would be used to determine the pressure of the water, as well as the current depth of the float, based on the density of the water. This depth could then be used in an algorithm to find neutral buoyancy, adjusting the syringe if it changed in a given time interval.

To communicate with the mission station, I wanted to use a Wi-Fi signal with a website interface. While this wasn’t ineffective, the nature of Wi-Fi’s high frequency meant that it could not penetrate through water easily. This meant that the float could only maintain a connection with the mission station while it was on the surface, and submerging to complete a vertical profile would break that connection. While workarounds were possible, an alternative could have been to use a microcontroller with a LoRa radio, like the Adafruit LoRa Radio FeatherWing. Long Range (LoRa) signals are designed to transmit small chunks of data across long distances, which would have been perfect for this scenario. Additionally, having a LoRa signal receiver on the mission station meant that we could avoid the hassle of having to connect and reconnect for every attempted profile.
## Conclusion and Future Improvements
The main obstacles in the completion of this project were time and a lack of manpower. While I had a design team for the float, due to other obligations, they were not able to assist. Taking rigorous courses like AP Calculus BC, AP Physics C: Mechanics, and AP Physics C: Electricity and Magnetism forced me to manage my time to contribute. Additionally, other team members were working on the ROV, which made up 75% of the competition. Therefore, I took it upon myself to focus on the float so that, at least, the float would be working well before the next competition.

With that being said, there are some glaring issues with this design that need to be addressed by future team members. One focal point of any vertical profiling float design is volume. Tiger Tower I was simply too big and did not respect the density of PVC, meaning that even with a large, 500 mL syringe, it would still need around 2.5 lbs of ballast to even reach neutral buoyancy. To put it succinctly, it either needs to be smaller or the chassis material needs to be replaced with something denser.

However, making the float smaller is easier said than done. While the electronics compartment won’t struggle with a decrease in size, the current linear actuator design would beg to differ. If we still wanted to commit to a custom-made linear actuator design, it would be best if, somehow, the length of the engine could decrease relative to the decrease in the chassis’ length. Additionally, the actuator’s design is too wide and should be streamlined to fit into a smaller diameter.

Finally, the Raspberry Pi Pico W can meet our requirements, but for the sake of convenience, it may be better to just buy two Adafruit LoRa Radio FeatherWings to make communication easier. This way, we can eliminate the need for an online interface and the need to re-establish a connection, only relying on choosing a certain frequency to receive.

Even though this project failed, I was able to gain a whole new insight into the process of designing a vertical profiling float and robotics in general, thanks to this competition. Our circumstances were dire, and yet our team managed to place 3rd in the Houston MATE ROV Competition, despite it being our first time competing, so I believe that our next team will have much more to offer. Additionally, doing research for this project took up a good chunk of time, and even now, many oversights could’ve been addressed sooner. I hope my findings are useful to future club members interested in building a vertical profiling float, as well as other students interested in this sort of technology.
